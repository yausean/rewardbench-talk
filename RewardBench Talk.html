RewardBench: testing the judge behind RLHF

RLHF trains a chatbot against a reward model. Until this paper, almost nobody measured whether that reward model actually picks the better answer.

Context

The reward model is where human preferences become a number

DPO shortcut: skip steps 2–3 and train the policy on the pairs directly. The reward is still there, implicitly, inside the policy.

Policies are benchmarked heavily. The reward model, which encodes whose values the policy ends up optimising, was barely evaluated at all.

Motivation

Why existing evaluations were not enough

60–70%

Old test sets hit a ceiling

Anthropic HH and Learning-to-Summarize cap out here because human annotators disagree with each other. A better RM can't show it.

0

New datasets lack test splits

UltraFeedback, UltraInteract and Nectar ship training data only. There was no held-out set to check a new RM against.

Indirect

Downstream evals test the policy

AlpacaEval, MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena score the final chatbot. A bad result can come from the RM, the base model or the RL settings.

The analogy I'll keep using

The reward model is a tutor marking essays; the policy is the student. Downstream evals grade the student's exam. RewardBench gives the tutor pairs of essays where we already know which is better, and checks the marking.

Contributions · Section 1

Four things the paper sets out to do

1

A common evaluation framework

One inference stack that scores classifiers, DPO models and LLM judges the same way. The authors also release the tools and every text–score pair from ~75 models for further analysis.

2

DPO vs classifier reward models

DPO models are more plentiful because DPO is simple to run. But they score very low on older preference test sets and their results vary more.

3

A map of current reward models

How today's reward models compare on scaling, their tendency to refuse, reasoning ability and more.

4

Limits of existing test sets

Older preference test sets are noisy and hit a ceiling. Reward models also fail on subtle pairs, such as a polished answer to the wrong prompt.

Background · Section 3

Two ways to get a reward

Classifier RM (Bradley–Terry, trained by MLE)

P(chosen ≻ rejected) = σ(rc − rr)
loss = log(1 + err − rc) = −log P

Only the difference matters. Shift both scores by the same amount and nothing changes, which is why RewardBench scores by ranking, not by raw value.

DPO model (implicit reward)

r(x,y) = β log π(y|x) ⁄ πref(y|x) + β log Z(x)

To compare two answers to the same prompt, Z(x) cancels, so the test is:

log π(y1|x)/πref(y1|x) > log π(y2|x)/πref(y2|x)
  • No separate RM: any DPO chat model can be scored as a judge.
  • Needs two forward passes: the policy and its exact reference model.
  • Outputs are large negative numbers (summed log-probs), not centred scores.

This lets the paper put classifiers and DPO models on one leaderboard for the first time.

Dataset · Section 4

Four sections, ~3,000 verified trios

Scoring

  • Accuracy per subset: share of trios where chosen beats rejected. Random = 50%.
  • Section score = per-prompt weighted mean of its subsets. Reasoning up-weights PRM Math so maths and code count equally.
  • Final score = mean of sections, with Prior Sets at 0.5 weight.
  • Length control: chosen answers are about as long as, or shorter than, rejected ones.

Scaling helps one family, not the other

Tülu 2 DPO (Llama 2)Qwen 1.5 Chat

Same training recipe at 7B → 13B → 70B gives steady gains for Tülu 2. Qwen 1.5 is flat, which the authors attribute to out-of-distribution generalisation.

DPO models are plentiful because they're cheap to train, but many releases don't document their reference model. Without it, they can't be benchmarked reliably. DPO models also score very low on the older preference test sets (Prior Sets), and their results vary more than classifiers'.

Finding 4 · Table 8

An 8B classifier edges out frontier LLM judges

Generative judges are prompted to compare two answers and pick one, often explaining first. That costs far more compute per comparison than one forward pass of a classifier.

Gemini 1.5 Pro leads on Chat Hard (80.6 vs 76.8), so the gap is narrow and section-dependent. Closed models sit on the public leaderboard but are excluded from the main paper as not reproducible.

Critique

The open question: does a better judge make a better student?

Limitations the authors state

  • No downstream link. RewardBench accuracy is not yet shown to predict post-RLHF policy quality.
  • Semi-automatic pairs. Most trios come from model outputs, filtered and verified by hand, not fresh human labels.
  • Contamination. Prompts reuse AlpacaEval and MT-Bench, which some models may have trained on.
  • Spurious cues. Formats in some domains (e.g. reasoning) may carry shortcuts.

Wrap-up

Takeaways

1

Test the judge, not just the student. RewardBench is the first shared benchmark and toolkit for reward models across classifiers, DPO and LLM judges, with all scores released for further study.

2

Easy is solved, subtle is not. Chat ≈ 95–99%; Chat Hard tops out near 77–81%, and safety behaviour varies wildly.

3

Ranking ≠ usefulness yet. The benchmark measures pairwise agreement; its link to better RLHF policies was left for follow-up work.
